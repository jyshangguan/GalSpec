How many broad-line components does the profile need?

A spike-and-slab ("toggle") prior on the wing amplitudes, tested on mocks whose answer is known — generated 2026-10-24 16:14 by develop/dev_jax/broad_line_components.py.

11 of 11 checks pass. Every mock gets the number of components the data need: the generating count where the components are separable, and the smaller sufficient count where they are not. The count does not move with the amplitude floor or with the number of live points, and the Bayes factors agree with it.

1. The scheme

Each broad line gets 4 components: one core, which is always present, and 3 wings whose amplitudes are drawn from a spike-and-slab prior. With probability p_off a wing amplitude is exactly zero, and it then contributes nothing at all — no flux, no normalisation, no kernel. The remaining probability maps onto a linear slab on (0, 1] relative to the core peak. Because both states own real prior volume, a fixed-dimension nested sampler finds them, and the number of components becomes a posterior rather than a choice.

p_off = 0.2, 0.5, 0.7   (wings 0, 1, 2: higher order, less likely)
sigma_c and every sigma_w : uniform 300 - 8000 km/s
dv_w                      : uniform ±6000 km/s
a wing counts as needed when its peak exceeds 3% of the core peak

Why the ladder is a prior and not a decision. The likelihood is exactly symmetric under exchanging two wings, so no likelihood term can say which of them is "wing 0". The ladder breaks the tie in prior probability, which shrinks the higher components without prejudging their widths or shifts. What it does not do is identify the labels: in the tests below the same physical wing turns up under index 0 or index 2 from one sample to the next, which is why every quantity in this report is label-invariant — the number of wings, and components sorted by width.

The decision rule. For each posterior sample count the wings that are on and above the 3%-of-core floor; the mode of that distribution is the minimum number of components the data need, and k_min = 1 + mode. The per-wing Bayes factor BF = (P_on/P_off) / prior odds is reported as the supporting evidence (the prior odds are divided out so runs with different p_off are comparable).

4. How much does the floor matter?

The floor is the one number in the rule that is a choice rather than a measurement, so the answer is shown against it. A rule worth using gives the same k_min across a factor of ten in the floor.

truthwings k_min at 1%k_min at 3%k_min at 5%k_min at 10% verdict
0 wings 0 1 1 1 1 stable
1 wing 1 2 2 2 2 stable

k_min is 1 + mode of the number of wings above that floor: a "stable" row means the answer does not depend on where the floor is put.

7. Caveats

  • Labels are not identified. Two wings of similar width can swap index from sample to sample; only the count and the width-sorted set are meaningful. Quoting "wing 1 = 4200 km/s" would be wrong roughly half the time.
  • An extra Gaussian is cheap. With 1% noise, an unneeded wing can still buy a little chi-square at ~1% of the core peak. That is what the floor is for, and why the floor sensitivity above is part of the result.
  • Live points matter. The 400-live-point rows are shown to be marginal: one of them converged to a point worse than the truth. Production runs use 1000 and report the ln Z error.
  • The widths are capped at 8000 km/s by design (the published two-component fit puts the wind at ~6930 km/s). A wing pinned to that cap in the real spectrum is reported by prior_dominated() rather than silently accepted.
