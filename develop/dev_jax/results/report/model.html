The model, in plain words

OverviewThe modelResultsGlossary

A fit is only as good as the model. This page explains every ingredient in plain words, and why the model has 21 free numbers rather than 4 or 400.

1. What the data are

A spectrum is light split by wavelength: the horizontal axis is wavelength (here in Ångström, 1 Å = 0.1 nm) and the vertical axis is flux, how much light arrives per unit wavelength. We use the SDSS spectrum of IRAS 07546+3928, a galaxy whose centre hosts a supermassive black hole.

  • redshift: the galaxy moves away from us, which stretches every wavelength by a factor (1 + z). We divide it out, so the plot is in the rest-frame — wavelengths as they would be measured next to the galaxy.
  • dereddening: dust in our own Milky Way dims and reddens the light. We correct for it using the measured colour excess of this line of sight.
  • pixel: the spectrum is sampled every 0.046 Å. That is much finer than the instrument can actually resolve (see lsf below), which is normal for SDSS and is why the model has to include the instrument.

3. How one line is described

A gaussian is the bell curve: three numbers (peak height, centre, width) describe it completely. Real line profiles are close enough to a sum of a few bell curves that this is standard practice. Each Balmer line therefore gets:

  • a narrow Gaussian (narrow-line) — gas moving at a few hundred km/s;
  • a broad Gaussian (broad-line) — gas moving at thousands of km/s, swirling around the black hole;
  • a fixed wavelength — the line's laboratory value — plus a velocity shift.

Widths are quoted in km/s rather than Å: that converts a wavelength width into the speed of the gas that produced it, which is the physically interesting quantity. The relation between the Gaussian width (σ) and the full width at half maximum (fwhm) is FWHM = 2.355 σ.

4. Why 21 numbers, not 60

Eleven components, each with 3–9 parameters, would be 60+ free numbers. We use two tricks, both standard in the field, to keep it at 21:

  • Ties. Some numbers are physically the same for several lines: all narrow lines share one velocity shift and one width; all broad Balmer lines share one shift and one width. A tie is simply a rule saying "this parameter equals that one", so the sampler only varies it once.
  • Atomic physics. The two lines of a doublet ([O III], [N II], [S II], [O I]) have fixed brightness ratios, fixed by quantum mechanics. We impose them, so a doublet costs one amplitude instead of two.

5. The instrument is part of the model

No spectrograph measures a line exactly: it smears it with its line-spread function (LSF). SDSS has a resolving-power R ≈ 2000, meaning it can separate two wavelengths that differ by only 1 part in 2000 — at 5000 Å that is 2.5 Å. If we ignore this smearing, the model is forced to reproduce the instrument's profile with the gas's velocity width, which gives nonsense (the fit runs to the prior edges). We therefore convolve the whole model with a Gaussian of that width before comparing to the data.

6. The priors: what we allow before looking at the data

A prior is the range a parameter is allowed to take. Ours are deliberately wide, and they are derived from the data's own flux range rather than from a preliminary fit — so nothing is hidden in a hand-tuned starting point:

  • every emission-line peak may be anywhere from 10-3 to twice the brightest pixel of the spectrum, with a log-uniform prior (equal probability per factor of ten, which is the right choice for a quantity spanning orders of magnitude);
  • narrow-line widths: 30–1000 km/s (FWHM 70–2400 km/s);
  • broad Balmer widths: 500–20000 km/s;
  • the continuum slope may be anything from steeply falling to steeply rising.

7. The same model, twice

The CPU model is an astropy Fittable1DModel. The GPU model is a jax class written to evaluate the identical arithmetic. Both are built by one function in develop/dev_jax/sdss_agn.py, from a shared list of components, so they cannot drift apart. Two tests show it: at the best-fit parameters the two models agree to 1.5×10-15 of the peak flux, i.e. to float64 rounding error; and at 25 random points drawn from the prior the two likelihood values agree to 3×10-5 out of ~105 — a relative difference of 3×10-10. The GPU path is not approximating anything.
